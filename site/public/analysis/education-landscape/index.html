What This Page Is

This is not about one school. This is about a system.

Brian Banks runs two charter schools in Detroit — MacDowell Prep and Purpose Charter Academy. Together they serve about 362 children. But Banks operates within a $288M+/year charter school system involving 62+ schools, 10 authorizers, and a regulatory framework that Michigan’s own Citizens Research Council has called structurally inadequate.

To understand how a 9-time convicted felon runs schools for children, you have to understand the system that makes it possible.

1. Who Runs Education in Detroit

Detroit’s children attend school in three parallel systems. None of them answer to the same authority.

SystemEnrollmentGovernance
DPSCD (traditional public)~48,000Elected school board, superintendent
Charter schools~30,00010 different authorizers, mostly universities
Out-of-district~27% of residentsSuburban public and charter schools

More than half of Detroit’s school-age children attend schools outside the traditional public district. The charter sector alone handles $288M+/year in state per-pupil funding — public money flowing through privately governed institutions with widely varying oversight.

Michigan allows universities, community colleges, intermediate school districts, and local school districts to authorize charter schools. Detroit’s 62+ charters are authorized by 10 different entities:

The geography tells the story: Most Detroit charter schools are authorized by institutions located 50-250 miles away. Grand Valley State University, the largest authorizer of Detroit charters, is in Grand Rapids — a 2.5-hour drive. Central Michigan University, which authorized MacDowell, is in Mt. Pleasant — 2.5 hours north.

These institutions collect a 3% fee on each school’s state aid allocation (~$288/pupil/year). For a university authorizing 30 Detroit schools, that’s roughly $2.6M/year in authorization revenue — from children they never see.

Source: Detroit PEER — K-12 Education System Overview (2025), MCCSA Annual Report 2023-24

Single-School Operators (Like Banks)

Self-managed single-school operators receive the least oversight of any category. With no parent organization, no network reporting, and an authorizer hundreds of miles away, these schools operate with near-total autonomy.

Banks is a self-managed operator. He is superintendent of both MacDowell and PCA. Purpose Group LLC (his private company) takes 72.67% of MacDowell’s revenue as a “management fee.” There is no external management company to provide a check — he is the management company.

Source: Detroit Free Press charter investigation (2014)

4. The Money — $288M+/Year Through Charters

Per-Pupil Funding

Michigan’s foundation allowance for FY 2024-25 is $9,608 per pupil. With 30,000 Detroit-resident charter students, that’s **$288M/year in state aid** flowing through charter schools — before federal and categorical funding.

Funding LayerPer PupilTotal (30,000 students)
State foundation only$9,608~$288M/year
Total revenue (incl. federal)$12,000-$15,000~$360-$450M/year

The Authorizer Fee Incentive

Authorizers collect 3% of each school’s state aid — approximately $288 per pupil per year.

AuthorizerEst. Detroit PortfolioEst. Annual Fee Revenue
GVSU~30 schools, ~8,000 students~$2.3M
CMU~9 schools, ~2,500 students~$720K
DPSCD7 schools, ~1,500 students~$430K
Others~20 schools, ~5,000 students~$1.4M

This creates a structural conflict of interest: authorizers are financially incentivized to authorize more schools and keep them open — regardless of performance. A university in Grand Rapids collects $2.3M/year for “overseeing” schools it rarely visits.

For-Profit CMOs Resist Disclosure

Michigan law requires charter school financial disclosure, but for-profit management companies claim private-entity status and resist detailed financial reporting. When a for-profit CMO takes 72.67% of a school’s revenue (as Purpose Group does with MacDowell), the public cannot see where that money goes.

The Detroit Free Press documented in 2014 that most Michigan charter companies do not follow financial disclosure law. The situation has not meaningfully changed.

Source: Michigan HB 6277 (FY 2024-25 budget), University of Arkansas funding study

The pattern: An operator receives per-pupil funding → extracts money through management fees, related-party contracts, or vendor self-dealing → academic outcomes collapse → school closes or is restructured → the children are displaced → the operator opens a new school.

Banks’s dissolve-and-reform pattern (documented in the LARA analysis) mirrors the broader systemic pattern. Detroit Delta Prep lasted 22 days. Banks’s entities dissolve and reform within months. The children bear the cost each time.

6. Special Education — The Hidden Sorting

Charter schools and DPSCD serve similarly poor, majority-Black populations. But they do not serve the same children.

MetricDPSCDDetroit Charters
Special education enrollment16-18%7-10%
Students with autism22x more likelyRare
Deep poverty (Black students)More concentratedLess concentrated
Overall poverty (FRL)~85%~87%

Children with autism are 22 times more likely to be in a DPSCD school than a charter school. Students with severe disabilities are concentrated in DPSCD and Wayne RESA center programs — not in charters.

This means charter schools receive similar per-pupil funding while serving a less costly student population. The money follows the child, but the most expensive children stay in the district.

When a charter school reports academic results, those results reflect a pre-sorted population — students whose families chose to enroll them, who do not have the most severe disabilities, and who are less likely to be in the deepest poverty. The comparison is not apples to apples.

Source: Chalkbeat (2018), CGCS Detroit Special Education Report

Michigan’s Charter Law — National Context

Michigan’s charter school law receives widely divergent ratings depending on who is measuring:

FrameworkMichigan RankAssessment
NAPCS Model Law (2022)30th of 45 statesWeak on authorizer accountability
Center for Education Reform (2024)4th of 45 statesStrong on growth and autonomy
Citizens Research Council (2020)Critical“No statutory oversight standards”

The gap between these ratings tells the story: Michigan’s law is excellent at enabling charter school growth and terrible at ensuring charter school accountability. The law allows rapid expansion (which CER rewards) while providing no statutory standards for authorizer oversight (which CRC criticizes).

Key Michigan charter law features:

  • No cap on university-authorized charters (cap expired 2014)
  • No statutory authorizer evaluation system (only 12 states require this)
  • No explicit oversight standards — authorizers define their own rigor
  • Weak conflict-of-interest law — only covers board member relatives (since 2012)
  • For-profit CMOs can claim private-entity status and resist disclosure
  • 2016 Detroit-specific law: Only accredited authorizers can open new Detroit charters

Source: NAPCS Model Law (2022), CER Scorecard (2024), CRC Report (2020)
